## Abstract

I joined the Global Health Engineering (GHE) group at ETH Zurich on 1 July 2021 as an Open Science Specialist, a role and job description I helped define myself. After five years of providing research data support in practice,  I share what embedded data stewardship looks like at the level of a single research group, and how we use our own operational data to understand the impact of our practices.

I argue that data stewards belong inside research groups, not only in central services. Professors are increasingly overburdened by an ever-evolving world of research data and research software management, and the support they need is inherently personal: every group member brings unique skills and gaps, which require targeted coaching rather than one-size-fits-all training. Our goal at GHE is not to enforce a single stack of tools and workflows, but to drive a cultural shift toward research that is open, transparent, and conducted with rigour, with computational reproducibility at the core of the researchers’ mindset.

To make this work visible and measurable, we are building the ghedata package: an openly shared collection of the group’s operational data, including our supervision table, LinkedIn statistics, Zenodo metadata, and GitHub usage statistics. Treated together, these sources give us what we call “research intelligence” (analogous to business intelligence): actionable insights that inform strategic decisions about supervision, publishing, and Open Science investments. Alongside this, I have developed a group-level data management strategy grounded in Open Research Data, supervised five ETH Domain ORD-funded projects, and started designing metrics for the value of data stewardship services.

I will share concrete examples, lessons learned, and the trade-offs of an embedded model, and invite discussion on how research groups, central services, and funders can collaborate on measuring what data stewardship actually changes.
